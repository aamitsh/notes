    01Randomness and the probability scale

    Probability measures how likely an event is. It is a measurement, like length or area, but of likelihood.

    • Random experiment: an action you can repeat, such as tossing a coin or rolling a die, where you know all the possible results but cannot tell in advance which one will occur.
    • Subjective probability is a personal judgement ("it looks like rain"). This chapter is about measuring probability objectively, from data or from reasoning.
    • Probability is measured on a scale from 0 to 1. It can also be written as a percentage: 0.75 means a 75% chance.
    ProbabilityDescriptionExample
    0ImpossibleGetting a number greater than 6 on a die
    Between 0 and ½Less likelyRolling a 3 on a die
    ½Even chance (equally likely)Getting heads when a coin is tossed
    Between ½ and 1More likelyDrawing a card numbered 2 to 10 from a deck of 52 (36 such cards)
    1CertainPicking a red sweet from a bag of only red sweets
    0 ≤ P(E) ≤ 1 for every event E

    Quick check

    1. Describe each event as impossible, less likely, even chance, more likely or certain: (i) Monday comes after Sunday, (ii) rolling a 1 on a die, (iii) getting tails on a coin, (iv) getting a number less than 6 on a die, (v) rolling a 7 on a die.
      Show answer
      (i) certain (ii) less likely (iii) even chance (iv) more likely, 5/6 (v) impossible.
    2. Can 1.5 or –0.2 be the probability of an event?
      Show answer
      No. A probability always lies from 0 to 1.
    3. The probability that a team wins is 0.75. What does this mean?
      Show answer
      There is a 75% chance of winning: it is more likely than not, but not certain.
    4. Why is tossing a coin a fair way to decide which team bats first?
      Show answer
      Heads and tails are equally likely (½ each) and nobody can predict or control the result, so both teams have the same chance.

    02Experimental probability and data

    Experimental probability is found from evidence: by repeating an experiment many times, or from recorded data. Each repetition is called a trial.

    Experimental probability = (number of times the event occurred) ÷ (total number of trials)this fraction is also called the relative frequency of the event
    Example. A die is rolled 50 times and lands on 4 exactly 8 times. The experimental probability (relative frequency) of a 4 is 8/50 = 0.16, or 16%.

    Using statistical data and samples

    • Population: the whole group we want to know about. Sample: the smaller group we actually collect data from.
    • The probability found from the sample is used to estimate numbers for the population:
      estimate = probability from the sample × size of the population.
    • The estimate is more reliable when the sample is larger and more representative (for example, students from different classes, not just one).
    Example. Of 50 students asked, 20 like mango best. P(mango) = 20/50 = 0.4. For a school of 1500 students, the estimate is 0.4 × 1500 = 600 students.

    Quick check

    1. A coin is tossed 200 times and shows heads 92 times. Find the experimental probability of tails.
      Show answer
      108/200 = 0.54.
    2. In a sample of 40 students, 9 prefer the Sports Club. The school has 800 students. Estimate how many prefer the Sports Club.
      Show answer
      9/40 × 800 = 180 students.
    3. A tyre company records 1000 cases: 20 tyres lasted less than 4000 km, 210 lasted 4001 to 9000 km, 325 lasted 9001 to 14000 km and 445 lasted more than 14000 km. Find the probability that a tyre lasts more than 14000 km, and the probability that it lasts between 4000 and 14000 km.
      Show answer
      445/1000 = 0.445; (210 + 325)/1000 = 0.535.
    4. A paper cup is tossed 100 times and lands on its side 72 times. What is the experimental probability that it does not land on its side?
      Show answer
      28/100 = 0.28.
    5. ★ A student estimates the favourite fruit of the whole school by asking only her own class. Give two reasons why the estimate may be unreliable.
      Show answer
      The sample is small, and it is not representative: one class may not have the same tastes as other classes or age groups.

    03Theoretical probability

    Theoretical probability uses reasoning instead of an experiment. It assumes that all the possible outcomes are equally likely (a fair situation).

    P(E) = (number of favourable outcomes) ÷ (number of possible outcomes)
    Examples. Rolling a 4 on a die: 1/6 ≈ 0.167. Picking the letter B from the word PROBABILITY: there are 2 Bs among 11 letters, so 2/11 ≈ 0.182.
    • Fair or unbiased: a fair coin is symmetrical, so there is no reason for one side to come up more often than the other.
    • Check for equally likely outcomes. In a bag of 3 red and 7 blue marbles, "red" and "blue" are not equally likely, but each of the 10 marbles is. So P(red) = 3/10.
    • "Not" events: count the outcomes that are left. With 3 red, 2 blue and 1 green ball, P(not red) = 3/6 = ½. Notice that P(red) + P(not red) = 1.
    Experimental probabilityTheoretical probability
    Based onActual trials or collected dataReasoning about equally likely outcomes
    Needs an experiment?YesNo
    Can it change?Yes, from one set of trials to anotherNo, it is fixed for a given situation

    Law of Large Numbers: as the number of trials increases, the experimental probability tends to get closer to the theoretical probability. With few trials the two can be quite different.

    Gambler's Fallacy: the mistaken belief that after a run of one result, the opposite result is "due". A coin or a die has no memory. Each toss or roll is independent: after six heads in a row, the probability of heads on the next toss is still ½.

    Quick check

    1. What is the probability of getting an even number when a fair die is rolled?
      Show answer
      3/6 = ½.
    2. A letter is picked at random from the word PROBABILITY. Find the probability that it is I.
      Show answer
      2/11.
    3. Ten cards numbered 1 to 10 are in a box. One is drawn at random. Find the probability that it is a prime number.
      Show answer
      2, 3, 5, 7: 4/10 = 2/5.
    4. The letters of the word PEACE are on cards. Find the probability that a card drawn at random is not an E.
      Show answer
      3/5.
    5. A die has shown 6 three times in a row. What is the probability of a 6 on the next roll?
      Show answer
      Still 1/6. Each roll is independent.
    6. ★ A die is rolled 12 times and shows a 3 three times. Compare the experimental and theoretical probabilities of a 3. What would you expect after 6000 rolls?
      Show answer
      Experimental: 3/12 = ¼. Theoretical: 1/6. They differ because 12 trials is very few. After 6000 rolls the experimental value should be close to 1/6 (about 1000 threes).
    7. ★ A number is chosen at random from 1 to 50. Find the probability that it is a multiple of 3 or of 5.
      Show answer
      Multiples of 3: 16. Multiples of 5: 10. Multiples of both (15, 30, 45) are counted twice: 16 + 10 – 3 = 23. P = 23/50.
    Got all 7 right?

    04Sample space and events

    • Outcome: one possible result of an experiment.
    • Sample space S: the list of all possible outcomes, written in curly brackets. Every outcome must be included, and none may be listed twice.
    • Sample size n(S): the number of elements in the sample space.
    • Event: any one outcome or group of outcomes. An event is a subset of the sample space.
    ExperimentSample space Sn(S)
    Tossing a coin{H, T}2
    Rolling a die{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}6
    Tossing two coins{HH, HT, TH, TT}4
    Tossing three coins{HHH, HHT, HTH, THH, HTT, THT, TTH, TTT}8
    A coin and a die together{H1, H2, H3, H4, H5, H6, T1, T2, T3, T4, T5, T6}12
    Result of a match{Win, Lose, Draw}3
    Events. For two coins, the event "at least one head" is E = {HH, HT, TH}, so P(E) = 3/4. For a die, the event "greater than 4" is E = {5, 6}, so P(E) = 2/6 = 1/3.
    • HT and TH are different outcomes. That is why "one head and one tail" has probability 2/4 = ½, not 1/3.
    • Choose a sample space that suits the question. {Rain, No Rain} is enough to ask "will it rain?", but to ask about the amount of rain you need {No Rain, Drizzle, Light Rain, Heavy Rain}.
    • Outcomes in a sample space need not be equally likely. If three coins are tossed and the number of heads is recorded, S = {0, 1, 2, 3}, but 1 and 2 are more likely than 0 and 3.

    Quick check

    1. Write the sample space for rolling a die and tossing a coin together. What is n(S)?
      Show answer
      {H1, H2, H3, H4, H5, H6, T1, T2, T3, T4, T5, T6}; n(S) = 12.
    2. A fair has 3 snacks (Samosa, Pakora, Bhaji) and 2 drinks (Chai, Lassi). How many snack-and-drink combinations are there? List the event 'Samosa is chosen'.
      Show answer
      3 × 2 = 6 combinations. Event: {Samosa–Chai, Samosa–Lassi}.
    3. Three coins are tossed. Find the probability of getting exactly two heads.
      Show answer
      HHT, HTH, THH: 3/8.
    4. Three coins are tossed and the number of heads is recorded. Which list is the sample space: {1, 2, 3}, {0, 1, 2}, {0, 1, 2, 3, 4} or {0, 1, 2, 3}?
      Show answer
      {0, 1, 2, 3}. The first two leave out possible results and the third includes 4, which cannot happen.
    5. ★ For the experiment in the previous question, find the probability of each outcome in {0, 1, 2, 3}.
      Show answer
      P(0) = 1/8, P(1) = 3/8, P(2) = 3/8, P(3) = 1/8.
    Got all 5 right?

    05Tree diagrams and two-step experiments

    A multi-step experiment is a series of trials, such as tossing a coin twice. A tree diagram lists all its outcomes: each branch is one possible result of a step, and each path from start to end is one complete outcome.

    • Counting outcomes: multiply the number of branches at each step. Two coins: 2 × 2 = 4. Three coins: 2 × 2 × 2 = 8. Two dice: 6 × 6 = 36.
    • Probability of a path: multiply the probabilities along its branches.
    • Probability of an event: add the probabilities of all the paths that make up the event.

    With and without replacement

    A basket has 4 red and 5 blue balls. Two balls are drawn one after the other.

    EventFirst ball put backFirst ball laid aside
    Red, then blue4/9 × 5/9 = 20/814/9 × 5/8 = 5/18
    Two blue balls5/9 × 5/9 = 25/815/9 × 4/8 = 5/18

    Without replacement, the second step has one ball fewer, so its probabilities change.

    Probability from areas

    If a point is equally likely to land anywhere in a region, P = (favourable area) ÷ (total area). For a circle of diameter 1 m inside a 3 m by 2 m rectangle: (π × 0.52) ÷ 6 = π/24 ≈ 0.13.

    Quick check

    1. A fair coin is tossed twice. Find the probability of getting one head and one tail.
      Show answer
      HT and TH: 2/4 = ½.
    2. Two dice are rolled. Find the probability that the sum is 9.
      Show answer
      (3,6), (4,5), (5,4), (6,3): 4/36 = 1/9.
    3. Basket A has one apple and two oranges; basket B has one banana and one mango. One fruit is picked at random from each. Find the probability of picking an apple and a banana.
      Show answer
      1/3 × 1/2 = 1/6.
    4. A box has 4 balls numbered 1 to 4. Two balls are drawn. How many outcomes are there (i) with replacement, (ii) without replacement?
      Show answer
      (i) 4 × 4 = 16   (ii) 4 × 3 = 12.
    5. ★ A box has 3 red, 4 black and 2 green pens. You pick a pen and put it back; then your friend picks one. Find the probability that both pens are the same colour.
      Show answer
      (3/9)2 + (4/9)2 + (2/9)2 = (9 + 16 + 4)/81 = 29/81.
    6. ★ A basket has 4 red and 5 blue balls. Two balls are drawn without replacement. Find the probability that they are of different colours.
      Show answer
      Red then blue: 4/9 × 5/8 = 5/18. Blue then red: 5/9 × 4/8 = 5/18. Total 5/9.
    7. ★ Two dice are rolled. Find the probability that the sum is a prime number greater than 5.
      Show answer
      Sums 7 and 11: (6 + 2)/36 = 2/9.

    ✓Common mistakes to avoid

    • A probability is never negative and never more than 1. If you get such an answer, recheck the counting.
    • Use the total number of outcomes, not the number of types. With 3 red and 5 blue balls, P(red) = 3/8, not ½.
    • HT and TH are different outcomes. So are (2, 5) and (5, 2) on two dice.
    • 'At least one' includes 'more than one'. At least one head with two coins means HH, HT or TH.
    • 'Between 2 and 6' means 3, 4 and 5 only.
    • Experimental probability depends on the data; theoretical probability does not. Say which one a question is asking for.
    • An estimate from a sample is only an estimate. It is not a certain value for the whole population.
    • Gambler's Fallacy: past tosses or rolls do not change the probability of the next one.
    • Without replacement, the totals change for the second draw: one item fewer.
    • Count repeated letters. In PROBABILITY there are 11 letters, with B and I each appearing twice.
