    How to use these notes

    01Work done by a constant force

    Work is done on an object when a force moves it in the direction of the force. Double the force, or double the distance, and the work doubles. Lifting 3 bags together needs 3 times the force; lifting 1 bag 3 times as high needs 3 times the distance. Either way, the work is 3 times as much.

    W = F × sforce × displacement in the direction of the force
    • Unit: the joule (J). 1 J is the work done when 1 N moves an object 1 m along the force. 1 J = 1 N m = 1 kg m² s⁻².
    • Work has no direction. It is a number with a sign (+ or −), even though force and displacement have directions.
    • Always name the force and the object: "work done by gravity on the ball", "work done by the girl on the bag". Several forces can act at once, each doing different work.
    • Lifting slowly at steady speed: your upward force equals the weight mg, so the work you do in lifting through height h is mgh.
    Worked example. A 5 kg bag is lifted slowly by 3 m. Force = mg = 5 × 10 = 50 N. Work done by the person = 50 × 3 = 150 J.

    Checkpoint questions

    1. Write 1 joule in terms of kg, m and s. Show the steps.
      Show answer
      1 J = 1 N × 1 m. Since 1 N = 1 kg m s⁻², 1 J = 1 kg m s⁻² × 1 m = 1 kg m² s⁻².
    2. Crane A lifts one 200 kg block by 5 m. Crane B lifts two such blocks together by 2.5 m. Which does more work?
      Show answer
      A: 200 × 10 × 5 = 10 000 J. B: 400 × 10 × 2.5 = 10 000 J. Equal work. Twice the force over half the distance gives the same work.
    3. A girl slowly lifts a 2 kg book from the floor onto a shelf 1.5 m high. (a) How much work does she do on the book? (b) Name another force doing work on the book, and how much.
      Show answer
      (a) mgh = 2 × 10 × 1.5 = 30 J. (b) Gravity does −30 J (it pulls down while the book moves up).
    4. Why must we always say which force does work, and on which object? Give an example of two forces doing different work on the same object at the same time.
      Show answer
      Because several forces act together, each doing its own work. Lifting the book above: the girl does +30 J while gravity does −30 J. Total work is zero, so the book's kinetic energy does not change.
    5. ★ "If I push a box with 50 N and it moves 4 m, the work done on the box is 200 J." When is this correct, and when is it incomplete?
      Show answer
      200 J is the work done by your push, as long as the box moves along the push. But other forces also work on the box. With 30 N of friction, friction does 30 × (−4) = −120 J, so the total work on the box is only 80 J.
    Got all 5 right?

    02Zero, positive and negative work

    The sign of the work depends only on how the force and the displacement point relative to each other.

    SituationWork done by the forceExample
    Force and displacement in the same directionPositive: object gains energyPushing a wheelchair; gravity on a falling apple
    Force and displacement oppositeNegative: object loses energyGoalkeeper stopping a ball; brakes; friction on a sliding box
    Force perpendicular to displacementZeroCarrying a box on a level path; the floor's upward push on a box sliding along it
    No displacementZeroPushing a wall; holding a barbell still
    • Feeling tired is not doing work. Pushing a wall uses your body's internal energy (muscles keep contracting), but the work done on the wall is zero.
    • Negative displacement trick: for a force opposing motion, take the displacement as negative. Goalkeeper: 200 N × (−0.15 m) = −30 J.
    • Third-law pairs: the ball does positive work on the goalkeeper while the goalkeeper does negative work on the ball.
    • Ball thrown up: gravity's work is negative going up and positive coming down. For a round trip back to the same height, the total work by gravity is zero.
    • Dumbbell: lifting it, the girl does positive work; lowering it slowly, she still pushes up while it moves down, so she does negative work.
    Worked example. A girl carries a 5 kg bag 20 m along a level corridor, then climbs 3 m of stairs. Level walk: her force is perpendicular to the motion, so her work = 0. Stairs: her work = 5 × 10 × 3 = 150 J. Gravity over the whole trip: −150 J.

    Checkpoint questions

    1. Give the sign of the work done: (a) by friction on a box sliding along the floor, (b) by your hands on a ball you catch, (c) by gravity on a ball rolling along a level floor, (d) by a rope pulling a bucket up a well.
      Show answer
      (a) Negative. (b) Negative. (c) Zero (gravity is perpendicular to the motion). (d) Positive.
    2. A weightlifter holds a 100 kg barbell above her head for 10 s. How much work does she do on it? Why does she still feel tired?
      Show answer
      Zero, because the barbell does not move. She tires because her muscles keep contracting and use up her body's internal (chemical) energy.
    3. A goalkeeper's hands move back 20 cm while stopping a ball with an average force of 150 N. Find the work done (a) by the goalkeeper on the ball, (b) by the ball on the goalkeeper.
      Show answer
      (a) 150 × (−0.20) = −30 J. (b) +30 J: the ball pushes the hands back in the direction they move.
    4. ★ A ball is thrown straight up and caught at the same height. Give the sign of gravity's work (a) going up, (b) coming down, (c) for the whole trip.
      Show answer
      (a) Negative. (b) Positive. (c) Zero: −mgh going up and +mgh coming down cancel, because it ends at the height it started.
    5. ★ A waiter carries a 2 kg tray at constant height for 15 m, then lowers it slowly by 0.5 m onto a table. Find the waiter's work on the tray (a) during the walk, (b) while lowering it.
      Show answer
      (a) Zero: his upward force is perpendicular to the horizontal motion. (b) He pushes up with 20 N while the tray moves 0.5 m down: 20 × (−0.5) = −10 J.
    Got all 5 right?

    03Work from a force–displacement graph

    The work done by a force equals the area under its force–displacement graph. This works even when the force changes along the way.

    • Constant force (flat line): rectangle, area = F × width.
    • Force rising or falling steadily to or from zero: triangle, area = ½ × base × height.
    • Force below the axis (opposite to the motion): that area counts as negative work.
    • Total work = sum of the pieces, with signs. Then use the work–energy theorem (Section 4) to get the kinetic energy and speed.
    Worked example. A 2 kg block has 6 J of kinetic energy at x = 0. Force: +10 N from 0–2 m, zero from 2–3 m, −5 N from 3–5 m. Work = 20 + 0 − 10 = 10 J. Final KE = 6 + 10 = 16 J. ½ × 2 × v² = 16, so v = 4 m s⁻¹.

    Common traps: a force that is falling but still positive still speeds the object up (only more slowly); forgetting the ½ for triangles; forgetting the object's starting kinetic energy.

    Checkpoint questions

    1. A force of 15 N acts on a box over 0.4 m in its direction. Sketch the graph and find the work from its area.
      Show answer
      A rectangle 15 N high and 0.4 m wide: 15 × 0.4 = 6 J.
    2. A force rises steadily from 0 to 12 N over the first 3 m. Find the work done.
      Show answer
      Triangle: ½ × 3 × 12 = 18 J.
    3. A force is 8 N from x = 0 to 3 m, then falls steadily to 0 at x = 5 m. Find the total work. If it acts on a 4 kg block starting from rest on a smooth floor, find the speed at 5 m.
      Show answer
      Rectangle 8 × 3 = 24 J, triangle ½ × 2 × 8 = 8 J, total 32 J. ½ × 4 × v² = 32, v² = 16, v = 4 m s⁻¹.
    4. ★ A force acts on a 3 kg block at rest on a smooth floor: it rises from 0 to 18 N over 0–2 m, stays at 18 N from 2–3 m, then falls to 0 at 5 m. (a) Find the work in each part. (b) Find the speed at 5 m. (c) Is the block slowing down between 3 m and 5 m?
      Show answer
      (a) 18 J, 18 J, 18 J (total 54 J). (b) ½ × 3 × v² = 54, v² = 36, v = 6 m s⁻¹. (c) No. The force is still in the direction of motion, so the block keeps speeding up, just more slowly.
    5. ★ A 4 kg block starts at rest. A force of +8 N acts from x = 0 to 3 m, then −6 N from 3 m to 6 m. (a) Find the kinetic energy at 3 m. (b) Find the speed at 6 m. (c) Where is it fastest?
      Show answer
      (a) 8 × 3 = 24 J. (b) Total work = 24 − 18 = 6 J; ½ × 4 × v² = 6, v² = 3, v = √3 ≈ 1.7 m s⁻¹. (c) At x = 3 m, where the force changes to opposing the motion.
    Got all 5 right?

    04Energy, the work–energy theorem and forms of energy

    Energy is the capacity to do work. A moving cricket ball can knock over a wicket; a raised flowerpot can damage something below. Each got its energy from work done on it: the fielder's throw, or the lifting of the pot.

    Work done on an object = change in its energy
    • Positive work on an object increases its energy; negative work decreases it.
    • It holds even when forces are not constant, and for systems of objects. It lets us skip the equations of motion.
    • Total work from all forces = change in kinetic energy. A 5 kg box pushed with 50 N over 4 m against 30 N friction: push +200 J, friction −120 J, so KE gained = 80 J.
    • In a carrom shot, the striker does positive work on the white coin (it gains energy) and the coin does negative work on the striker (it loses energy). Energy is transferred.
    • Energy also moves as heat (hot to cold), by radiation (Sun to Earth), in electric circuits and through sound waves.
    Form of energyWhat it is
    MechanicalDue to motion (kinetic) or position/shape (potential)
    ThermalMakes things warm or hot
    LightAllows us to see
    SoundVibrations of air or other molecules
    ElectricalRelated to position or motion of charges
    ChemicalStored in bonds in food and fuels
    NuclearStored in the nuclei of atoms

    Common conversions: bulb (electrical → light + heat); heater (electrical → thermal); muscles (chemical → mechanical); bell (mechanical → sound); solar panel (light → electrical); gharat or water mill (potential → kinetic → turning wheel); dam (potential → kinetic → electrical).

    Checkpoint questions

    1. State the work–energy theorem. Why is it useful when a force keeps changing?
      Show answer
      Work done on an object equals the change in its energy. With a changing force we only need the total work (for example, the area under the graph), not the acceleration at every instant.
    2. Name the energy change in: (a) an unwinding watch spring, (b) photosynthesis, (c) a burning matchstick, (d) speaking into a microphone, (e) a truck moving uphill.
      Show answer
      (a) Potential (spring) → kinetic. (b) Light → chemical. (c) Chemical → heat + light. (d) Sound → electrical. (e) Chemical (fuel) → kinetic + gravitational potential (plus heat).
    3. A striker hits a white coin, which hits a black coin. Who does positive work on whom, and who does negative work on whom?
      Show answer
      Striker → white coin: positive. White coin → striker: negative. White coin → black coin: positive. Black coin → white coin: negative.
    4. ★ A 4 kg trolley at rest is pushed with 30 N over 5 m against 10 N of friction. Find (a) work by the push, (b) work by friction, (c) KE gained, (d) final speed.
      Show answer
      (a) 30 × 5 = 150 J. (b) −10 × 5 = −50 J. (c) 150 − 50 = 100 J. (d) ½ × 4 × v² = 100, v² = 50, v ≈ 7.1 m s⁻¹.
    5. ★ Describe every energy change in a gharat, from water at the top of the pipe to grain being ground.
      Show answer
      Water at the top has gravitational potential energy → it flows down the pipe and gains kinetic energy → it strikes and turns the wheel (kinetic energy of rotation) → the wheel turns the grinding stone, which does work on the grain (some energy also becomes heat and sound).
    Got all 5 right?

    05Kinetic energy

    Kinetic energy is the energy an object has because it is moving. An object at rest has zero kinetic energy.

    Derivation (constant force). From v² = u² + 2as, s = (v² − u²)/2a. Then W = F × s = ma × (v² − u²)/2a = ½mv² − ½mu².

    K = ½ m v²W = ½mv² − ½mu² (work done = change in kinetic energy)

    Reasoning with ratios (the most-tested skill)

    • Speed doubles → KE × 4. Speed triples → KE × 9. KE depends on the square of the speed.
    • Percentages: speed down 20% → new speed 0.8v → KE × 0.64 → KE falls by 36%, not 20%. Speed up 20% → KE × 1.44 (up 44%).
    • Equal KE, different masses: v ∝ 1/√m. Mass 4 times → speed half. Truck 9 times the car's mass → car 3 times as fast.
    • Same speed: KE ∝ m.
    • Unequal stages: 10 → 20 m s⁻¹ needs ½m(400 − 100); 20 → 30 m s⁻¹ needs ½m(900 − 400). The second stage needs 5/3 as much work.

    Stopping distance with a constant force

    F × s = KE, so s = KE ÷ F
    • Same KE and same force → same stopping distance, whatever the masses.
    • Same force, speed doubled → KE × 4 → distance × 4.
    • Average resisting force = KE ÷ distance (bullet in wood, coconut in sand, jet on an aircraft carrier).
    Worked example. A 20 g bullet at 300 m s⁻¹ stops in 6 cm of wood. KE = ½ × 0.02 × 300² = 900 J. Force = 900 ÷ 0.06 = 15 000 N.

    Unit trap: divide km h⁻¹ by 3.6 to get m s⁻¹ (72 → 20, 54 → 15, 36 → 10, 108 → 30).

    Checkpoint questions

    1. Derive K = ½mv² from v² = u² + 2as and W = F × s. What must you assume?
      Show answer
      Assume a constant force (so constant acceleration). Starting from rest, s = v²/2a, so W = ma × v²/2a = ½mv². This work is stored as kinetic energy.
    2. A 0.2 kg cricket ball is bowled at 108 km h⁻¹. Find its kinetic energy.
      Show answer
      108 km h⁻¹ = 30 m s⁻¹. K = ½ × 0.2 × 900 = 90 J.
    3. A (mass m) and B (mass 9m) have equal kinetic energy. Find the ratio of their speeds. If instead they had the same speed, find the ratio of their kinetic energies.
      Show answer
      m v_A² = 9m v_B², so v_A : v_B = 3 : 1. Same speed: KE_A : KE_B = 1 : 9.
    4. A scooter's speed falls by 50%. By what percentage does its kinetic energy fall?
      Show answer
      New KE = (0.5)² = 0.25 of the old, so it falls by 75%.
    5. ★ A car goes from 0 to 10 m s⁻¹, then from 10 to 20 m s⁻¹. Find the ratio of the engine's work in the second stage to the first (no friction).
      Show answer
      Second: ½m(400 − 100) = 150m. First: ½m(100 − 0) = 50m. Ratio 3 : 1.
    6. ★ A 1500 kg car at 72 km h⁻¹ brakes with a constant 6000 N. (a) Find the stopping distance. (b) At 36 km h⁻¹ instead, what is it? Answer (b) by ratio.
      Show answer
      (a) v = 20 m s⁻¹, KE = ½ × 1500 × 400 = 300 000 J, s = 300 000 ÷ 6000 = 50 m. (b) Half the speed → a quarter of the KE → 12.5 m.
    7. ★ When a body's speed rises by 2 m s⁻¹, its kinetic energy rises by 21%. Find its initial speed.
      Show answer
      (v + 2)² = 1.21v², so v + 2 = 1.1v, giving v = 20 m s⁻¹.
    Got all 7 right?

    06Potential energy

    Potential energy is energy stored in an object because of its shape, or in a system of objects because of their relative positions.

    • Stored by shape: a stretched slingshot, a bent bow, a compressed spring. The work done to deform it is stored; when released, it returns to shape and gives kinetic energy to the stone or arrow.
    • Stored by position: unlike magnetic poles pulled apart, or charges held apart. Released, they rush together and gain kinetic energy.
    • Earth–ball system: lifting a ball stores gravitational potential energy. The Earth hardly moves, so we call it "the ball's potential energy".
    U = m g hvalid near the Earth's surface; h measured from a chosen zero level
    • The path does not matter, only the vertical height. A 50 kg student reaching a 72.5 m top floor gains 50 × 10 × 72.5 = 36 250 J by lift or by stairs.
    • Horizontal motion at constant height does not change U.
    • Only near the surface: far from the Earth, g gets smaller and U = mgh no longer holds.
    • Work against friction is not stored as potential energy. It cannot be got back.
    • Sand-pit activity: a ball dropped from higher makes a deeper dent, because it started with more potential energy.

    Checkpoint questions

    1. Give two examples of potential energy stored by shape and two stored by relative position.
      Show answer
      Shape: bent bow, compressed spring (or stretched slingshot band). Position: a ball lifted above the Earth; two unlike magnet poles (or charges) held apart.
    2. A 200 g ball is thrown up to 10 m above the ground. Find its potential energy at the top.
      Show answer
      0.2 × 10 × 10 = 20 J.
    3. A crane lifts a mass to the 10th floor, then the same mass to the 20th floor (equal floor heights). Compare the energy needed.
      Show answer
      The height doubles, so the 20th floor needs twice the energy (as much again). If it takes twice the time, the power is the same (Section 8).
    4. ★ One girl walks up a long winding ramp to the top of a 12 m tower; her friend of equal mass climbs a vertical ladder. Compare their gains in potential energy.
      Show answer
      Equal: both gain mgh with h = 12 m. Potential energy depends only on vertical height, not on the path.
    5. ★ Why does lifting a box store energy, while dragging it across a rough floor does not?
      Show answer
      Released from a height, the box gives back the energy as kinetic energy as gravity pulls it down. Work against friction turns into heat in the surfaces and cannot be got back, so it is not stored.
    6. ★ A 1 kg book is on a 1 m high table. Find its PE (a) with the floor as zero, (b) with the table top as zero. Does the change in PE when it falls to the floor depend on this choice?
      Show answer
      (a) 10 J. (b) 0 J. Falling to the floor, the change is −10 J either way. No: only changes in PE matter.
    Got all 6 right?

    07Conservation of mechanical energy, and when energy is lost

    Mechanical energy = kinetic energy + potential energy. When only gravity does work, it stays constant: potential energy lost becomes kinetic energy, and the reverse.

    ½mu² + mgh₁ = ½mv² + mgh₂

    Proof for free fall. Dropped from h, after time t: v = gt and height h′ = h − ½gt². KE = ½mg²t², PE = mgh − ½mg²t². Their sum is always mgh.

    Tools for problems

    • Frictionless slide: v = √(2gh). The same for any mass and any shape of slide; only the height matters.
    • With a starting speed u: v² = u² + 2gh. Pushed at 3 m s⁻¹ down a 2 m slide: v² = 9 + 40 = 49, v = 7 m s⁻¹.
    • Maximum height when thrown up at u: h = u²/2g (10 m s⁻¹ → 5 m).
    • Sharing energy (dropped from H): at height y, PE = mgy and KE = mg(H − y). KE = PE at H/2. KE = 3 × PE at H/4. At H/3, KE : PE = 2 : 1.
    • Pendulum: all PE at the ends, all KE at the lowest point. Real pendulums slow down because of friction at the support and air resistance.
    • Potential-energy tracks: total energy = PE at the start (if starting at rest). The object reaches points where PE ≤ total, stops where PE = total, and can never reach PE > total.

    When friction or air resistance act

    (final KE + PE) − (initial KE + PE) = work by friction or air resistancethis work is negative, so mechanical energy falls
    • Air resistance: 0.5 kg thrown up at 20 m s⁻¹ reaches only 16 m. Start 100 J, top 80 J, so air did −20 J.
    • Escape ramp: the KE goes into PE gained plus work against the sand. A 2000 kg car at 20 m s⁻¹ (400 000 J), ramp rising 1 m per 4 m, gravel 3000 N: 400 000 = 5000d + 3000d, so d = 50 m.
    • Rebound: dropped from 20 m, bounces to 1.8 m. Speed just after the bounce = √(2 × 10 × 1.8) = 6 m s⁻¹. Energy was lost in the impact.
    • Science-park roller coaster: each later hump is lower, because friction removes some energy each time.

    Checkpoint questions

    1. Show, using v = gt and h′ = h − ½gt², that a falling body's mechanical energy is mgh at every instant.
      Show answer
      KE = ½m(gt)² = ½mg²t². PE = mg(h − ½gt²) = mgh − ½mg²t². Sum = mgh, independent of t.
    2. Children of 20 kg and 40 kg slide from rest down the same frictionless slide. Compare their speeds at the bottom. Would a steeper slide of the same height change this?
      Show answer
      Equal speeds, v = √(2gh). Mass cancels. A steeper slide of the same height gives the same speed.
    3. A ball is dropped from 45 m. At what height is its KE (a) equal to its PE, (b) twice its PE?
      Show answer
      (a) 45 ÷ 2 = 22.5 m. (b) Total = 3 × PE, so PE = ⅓ of total: 15 m.
    4. ★ A 0.2 kg pendulum bob is released from 45 cm above its lowest point. Find (a) its speed at the lowest point, (b) its speed 25 cm above the lowest point, (c) its mechanical energy.
      Show answer
      (a) v² = 2 × 10 × 0.45 = 9, 3 m s⁻¹. (b) It has fallen 0.20 m: v² = 4, 2 m s⁻¹. (c) 0.2 × 10 × 0.45 = 0.9 J.
    5. ★ A 0.5 kg ball starts at rest on a frictionless track where its PE is 40 J. Find its speed where PE = 15 J. Can it reach a point where PE = 45 J? Where does it stop?
      Show answer
      KE = 25 J, ½ × 0.5 × v² = 25, v² = 100, v = 10 m s⁻¹. It cannot reach PE = 45 J (more than its 40 J total). It stops momentarily wherever PE = 40 J.
    6. ★ A 3 kg stone dropped from 20 m hits the ground at 18 m s⁻¹. Find the work done by air resistance.
      Show answer
      Start: PE = 600 J. End: KE = ½ × 3 × 324 = 486 J. Work by air = 486 − 600 = −114 J.
    7. ★ A 1000 kg truck at 20 m s⁻¹ enters an escape ramp that rises 1 m for every 2 m along it. Sand exerts 15 000 N against the motion. Find the length of ramp needed.
      Show answer
      KE = 200 000 J. Over distance d: PE gained = 1000 × 10 × d/2 = 5000d; sand's work = 15 000d. 200 000 = 20 000d, d = 10 m.
    8. ★ A 1 kg ball dropped from 5 m rebounds to 3.2 m. Find (a) its speed just before impact, (b) just after, (c) the energy lost.
      Show answer
      (a) √(2 × 10 × 5) = 10 m s⁻¹. (b) √(2 × 10 × 3.2) = 8 m s⁻¹. (c) 50 J − 32 J = 18 J.
    Got all 8 right?

    08Power

    Power is the rate at which work is done. Running up the stairs and walking up them need the same work, but running needs more power.

    P = W ÷ t1 W = 1 J s⁻¹; 1 hp = 746 W
    • More work in the same time, or the same work in less time, means more power.
    • How fast you do a job doesn't change the work. Raising a flag slowly or quickly needs the same work (mgh). Twice as fast needs twice the power.

    Recipe: find the work first, then divide by the time

    • Lifting (cranes, weightlifters, stairs): W = mgh. 75 kg lifted 2 m in 5 s → 1500 J ÷ 5 s = 300 W.
    • Speeding up (car engines): W = change in KE. 1000 kg car, 0 → 20 m s⁻¹ in 10 s → 200 000 J ÷ 10 s = 20 kW.
    • Pumps: W = PE given to the water + KE of the water leaving the pipe. 600 kg lifted 15 m, leaving at 2 m s⁻¹, in 300 s → (90 000 + 1200) ÷ 300 = 304 W.
    • Mass lifted per second by a pump of power P (slow outflow): P ÷ (g × h).
    • Comparing machines: find each power separately. More work does not mean more power.

    Checkpoint questions

    1. Define power and its SI unit. Convert 15 kW to horsepower (nearest whole number).
      Show answer
      Power = work ÷ time, in watts (J s⁻¹). 15 000 ÷ 746 ≈ 20 hp.
    2. A 50 kg girl climbs 4 m of stairs in 8 s. Her 60 kg brother climbs the same stairs in 12 s. Who does more work? Who has more power?
      Show answer
      Girl: 2000 J, 250 W. Brother: 2400 J, 200 W. The brother does more work; the girl has more power.
    3. A crane lifts a 400 kg load 15 m in 30 s. Find its power. If it does the same lift in 15 s, what happens to (a) the work, (b) the power?
      Show answer
      60 000 J ÷ 30 s = 2000 W. (a) Work stays 60 000 J. (b) Power doubles to 4000 W.
    4. ★ An 800 kg car accelerates from rest to 54 km h⁻¹ in 6 s on a level road. Find the engine's average power in kW and hp, ignoring losses.
      Show answer
      v = 15 m s⁻¹, KE = ½ × 800 × 225 = 90 000 J. P = 90 000 ÷ 6 = 15 kW ≈ 20 hp.
    5. ★ A pump lifts 1200 kg of water 10 m in 4 minutes; the water leaves the pipe at 5 m s⁻¹. Find the pump's power, ignoring losses.
      Show answer
      PE = 1200 × 10 × 10 = 120 000 J. KE = ½ × 1200 × 25 = 15 000 J. Total 135 000 J ÷ 240 s = 562.5 W.
    6. ★ A 1.2 kW pump lifts water to a tank 15 m above it. How many kg of water does it lift each second (slow outflow)?
      Show answer
      1200 ÷ (10 × 15) = 8 kg per second.
    Got all 6 right?

    09Simple machines, mechanical advantage and pulleys

    Simple machines make a task easier by changing the size or the direction of the force you apply. They never reduce the total work needed.

    • Effort: the force we apply to the machine. Load: the force to be overcome.
    Mechanical advantage (MA) = load ÷ effort
    • MA > 1: a small effort moves a big load, but the effort moves through a bigger distance.
    • Work is the same (ignoring friction): effort × its distance = load × its distance. A machine with MA = 4 needs a quarter of the force, over 4 times the distance.
    • Fixed pulley: a grooved wheel at the top that guides a rope. Effort = load, so MA = 1. It helps only by changing direction: pulling down (using your body weight) is easier than lifting up. Used for flags and wells.
    • Movable pulley / pulley systems: the load hangs from a movable pulley; one rope end is fixed. These can have MA greater than 1 (cranes, lifts).
    • No perpetual motion machine: machines do not create energy. Friction always turns some energy into heat, so a real machine slows down and stops unless energy keeps being supplied.

    Checkpoint questions

    1. A machine lifts a 900 N load with a 300 N effort. Find its MA. If the load rises 1 m, how far must the effort move (no friction)?
      Show answer
      MA = 900 ÷ 300 = 3. Work is the same: 300 × d = 900 × 1, so d = 3 m.
    2. A fixed pulley has MA = 1. Why use it at all?
      Show answer
      It changes the direction of the effort. Pulling down is easier and safer than lifting up, and you can use your own body weight.
    3. True or false: "A machine with MA = 4 lets you do one quarter of the work." Explain.
      Show answer
      False. The effort is a quarter, but it moves 4 times as far, so the work is the same (ignoring friction).
    4. ★ A 0.5 kg flag is raised 12 m using a fixed pulley at steady speed. Find (a) the effort, (b) the work done. (c) Find the power if it takes 10 s, and if it takes 20 s.
      Show answer
      (a) Effort = load = 0.5 × 10 = 5 N. (b) 5 × 12 = 60 J. (c) 6 W in 10 s; 3 W in 20 s. Same work, different power.
    5. ★ Many perpetual motion machines have been designed, yet none works. Explain why using work and energy.
      Show answer
      A machine cannot create energy; the work it gives out is at most the work put in. Friction and air resistance turn some energy into heat in every cycle, so without a supply of energy it must slow down and stop.
    Got all 5 right?

    10Inclined plane

    An inclined plane (ramp) lets you raise a heavy load with a smaller force, by pushing it over a longer distance.

    Derivation. Pushing the load slowly up a smooth ramp, work done by you = F′ × L. Potential energy gained = mgh. By the work–energy theorem, F′ × L = mgh.

    MA = load ÷ effort = mg ÷ F′ = L ÷ h
    • L is always greater than h, so the MA of a ramp is always more than 1 and the effort is less than the weight.
    • Longer, gentler ramp → smaller effort but a longer push. The work (mgh) is the same for every ramp to the same height.
    • Use the slope length L, not the horizontal base. If you are given the height and the horizontal distance, find L by Pythagoras: L = √(h² + base²). A 3-4-5 triangle shows up often (0.3, 0.4, 0.5 m; 1.2, 1.6, 2.0 m; 1.5, 2.0, 2.5 m).
    • Everyday examples: hill roads wind around in gentle slopes (smaller force needed by vehicles); an inclined ladder is easier to climb than a vertical one; ramps for wheelchairs and loading trucks.
    Worked example. A ramp reaches a 1.2 m platform from a point 1.6 m away horizontally. L = √(1.44 + 2.56) = 2.0 m. Effort to push a 600 N box = 600 × 1.2 ÷ 2.0 = 360 N. MA = 2.0 ÷ 1.2 ≈ 1.67.

    Checkpoint questions

    1. Derive MA = L/h for a smooth inclined plane using the work–energy theorem.
      Show answer
      Work by effort = F′ × L. Energy gained by the load = mgh. So F′ × L = mgh, and MA = mg/F′ = L/h.
    2. A 6 m ramp reaches a 1.5 m high platform. Find its MA and the effort needed for an 800 N load.
      Show answer
      MA = 6 ÷ 1.5 = 4. Effort = 800 ÷ 4 = 200 N.
    3. A ramp's foot is 2.4 m horizontally from a 1.8 m high platform. Find (a) the ramp length, (b) its MA, (c) the effort for a 900 N load.
      Show answer
      (a) √(1.8² + 2.4²) = √9 = 3.0 m. (b) 3.0 ÷ 1.8 ≈ 1.67. (c) 900 × 1.8 ÷ 3.0 = 540 N.
    4. ★ A 500 N box is pushed up a smooth plank to a height of 0.8 m with an effort of 125 N. Find the plank's length, and check that the work done by the effort equals the PE gained.
      Show answer
      L = 500 × 0.8 ÷ 125 = 3.2 m. Work by effort = 125 × 3.2 = 400 J; PE gained = 500 × 0.8 = 400 J. ✓
    5. ★ Explain why roads in hills wind around in gentle slopes, and why an inclined ladder is easier to climb than a vertical one.
      Show answer
      Both are long inclined planes. Spreading the same rise over a longer path makes L/h larger, so the force needed at each moment is smaller. The total work (mgh) is the same; it is just spread over a longer distance.
    6. ★ A 500 N load is raised 1 m using ramp A (2 m long) or ramp B (5 m long). Find the effort and the work done for each. Which is easier, and which needs less work?
      Show answer
      A: 250 N × 2 m = 500 J. B: 100 N × 5 m = 500 J. Ramp B is easier (less force), but both need the same work.
    Got all 6 right?

    11Levers

    A lever is a rigid bar that turns about a fixed point. It has three parts: the fulcrum (the fixed turning point), the load (the force to be overcome) and the effort (the force applied).

    effort × effort arm = load × load armMA = load ÷ effort = effort arm ÷ load arm
    • Distances moved: each end moves in proportion to its arm. If the effort arm is 5 times the load arm, the effort end moves 5 times as far as the load. F₁ × d₁ = F₂ × d₂, so the work is the same at both ends.
    • Measure arms from the fulcrum, not from the end of the bar. On a 1.2 m rod with the fulcrum 0.2 m from the load, the effort arm is 1.0 m, not 1.2 m.
    • Seesaw: balanced when mass × distance is equal on both sides (15 kg at 2 m balances 30 kg at 1 m). Two children on one side: add their mass × distance.
    • Beam balance (Activity 7.5): balanced when n₁ × L₁ = n₂ × L₂ (number of coins × distance). The heavier pan must sit closer to the centre.

    Classes of levers

    ClassWhat is in the middleExamples (from the chapter)MA
    IFulcrumScissors, crowbar, pliers, beam balance, seesawMore than, less than or equal to 1
    IILoadLemon squeezer, wheelbarrow, bottle openerAlways more than 1 (effort arm is longer)
    IIIEffortTweezers, broom, hammer, oarAlways less than 1 (effort arm is shorter)

    Many machines combine two or more simple machines. In every case the work put in equals the useful work done on the load, ignoring friction.

    Checkpoint questions

    1. A crowbar is used with an effort of 60 N applied 1.5 m from the fulcrum. The rock is 0.25 m from the fulcrum. Find the load it can lift and the MA.
      Show answer
      Load = 60 × 1.5 ÷ 0.25 = 360 N. MA = 1.5 ÷ 0.25 = 6.
    2. A seesaw has seats at 1 m, 1.5 m and 2 m from the fulcrum on each side. A 25 kg child sits at 1.5 m. Where should a 37.5 kg child sit? Can a 50 kg adult balance on any seat?
      Show answer
      25 × 1.5 = 37.5 × d gives d = 1 m. For 50 kg, d = 0.75 m, and there is no seat there, so no.
    3. On a beam balance, 2 coins at 30 cm balance 6 coins on the other side. Where are the 6 coins? If one more coin is added to that pan, where must it move?
      Show answer
      2 × 30 = 6 × d, d = 10 cm. With 7 coins: d = 60 ÷ 7 ≈ 8.6 cm (closer to the centre).
    4. ★ A light rod 1.5 m long has its fulcrum 0.3 m from a 900 N load at one end. The effort is at the other end. Find the effort. If the effort end moves down 20 cm, how far does the load rise? Check that the work is equal.
      Show answer
      Effort arm = 1.2 m. Effort = 900 × 0.3 ÷ 1.2 = 225 N. Load rises 20 × 0.3 ÷ 1.2 = 5 cm. Check: 225 × 0.20 = 45 J and 900 × 0.05 = 45 J. ✓
    5. ★ Classify each lever and say whether its MA is more than 1, less than 1, or could be either: wheelbarrow, tweezers, scissors, bottle opener, broom.
      Show answer
      Wheelbarrow: II, more than 1. Tweezers: III, less than 1. Scissors: I, either. Bottle opener: II, more than 1. Broom: III, less than 1.
    6. ★ Why do you push a hard object close to the joint of a pair of scissors to cut it? Why is a spoon handle useful for opening a tight lid?
      Show answer
      Scissors: moving the object nearer the fulcrum shortens the load arm, so the MA rises and the blades press harder for the same effort. Spoon: it acts as a lever with the rim as the fulcrum. Your hand is far from it (long effort arm) and the lid is very close (short load arm), so a small effort gives a large force on the lid.

    ✓Common traps before the test

    • Sign of work: forces opposing the motion (friction, air resistance, brakes, a catcher's hands) do negative work. Perpendicular forces do zero work.
    • Graphs: include the ½ for triangles; areas below the axis are negative; add the object's starting KE.
    • KE scales with v²: a 20% drop in speed is a 36% drop in KE.
    • Equal KE + equal force → equal stopping distance, however different the masses.
    • Units: km h⁻¹ ÷ 3.6 = m s⁻¹; grams → kg; cm → m; minutes → seconds.
    • Potential energy is path-independent; only the vertical height counts.
    • Losses: with friction or air resistance, use (final KE + PE) − (initial KE + PE) = work by those forces.
    • Pumps: add the KE of the water leaving the pipe if it has a speed.
    • Ramps: MA = slope length ÷ height. Never use the horizontal base as L.
    • Levers: measure the arms from the fulcrum; the load end moves less when it is closer to the fulcrum.
    • No machine reduces the total work. A bigger MA means a smaller force over a longer distance.
